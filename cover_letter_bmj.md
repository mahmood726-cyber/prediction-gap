Dear Editor,

We submit for your consideration the manuscript **"The Prediction Gap: 70% of Statistically Significant Cochrane Meta-Analyses Have Prediction Intervals That Include the Null"** as a Research Article in *The BMJ*.

**The problem:** Clinical guidelines and GRADE assessments rely on the confidence interval to judge whether a treatment "works." But the confidence interval answers only "how precisely have we estimated the average effect?" — not "will this treatment work in the next clinical setting?" The prediction interval answers the latter question, yet is almost never reported.

**What we found:** Across 403 Cochrane systematic reviews, 70% of statistically significant meta-analyses had prediction intervals that included the null. Only 14% of all reviews provided concordant evidence that the treatment effect would apply in a new setting. Prediction intervals were on average 3.1 times wider than confidence intervals. The false reassurance rate increased sharply with heterogeneity — from 42% at low heterogeneity to 95% at moderate-to-high heterogeneity — but was already substantial even when heterogeneity appeared low.

**Why this matters for BMJ readers:** This finding challenges the standard interpretation of meta-analytic significance. A clinician reading "RR 0.85, 95% CI 0.78-0.93" is given false reassurance when the prediction interval is 0.55-1.31. The practical implication is immediate: routine reporting of prediction intervals should become standard in systematic reviews, and GRADE assessments should consider prediction interval width alongside confidence interval width when rating the certainty of evidence.

**Novelty:** While Riley et al. (2011) and IntHout et al. (2016) advocated for prediction intervals theoretically and in small samples, no previous study has quantified the CI-PI discordance at this scale across hundreds of real Cochrane meta-analyses. Our finding that the discordance rate is 70% — not 20% or 30% — makes the case for policy change considerably more urgent.
